Where should a defender place a few traps (honeytoken credentials, canary objects, high-fidelity sensors) on the nodes of an Active Directory domain? We model an attacker who collects several assets under a time budget and risks detection at every step: a surviving-orienteering problem on the attack graph extracted from BloodHound data, where a pivot-switch cost δ interpolates between credential accumulation (δ = 0) and a single route (δ = ∞). Trap placement is a Bayesian zero-sum game over unknown footholds. An index rule yields a knapsack completion bound valid for every δ and every trap mixture; since marginal loot depends only on the acquired set, the attacker’s best response becomes an exact dynamic program. With exhaustive enumeration of trap sets on the defender side, a double oracle returns game values whose certificates are checked without any MILP or LP solver. On 11 synthetic domains with redundant paths (81–141 nodes), the certified optimal randomized deception leaves the attacker 11.6% of the undefended loot, against 16.3% for the best fixed placement, 39.9% for the best of three heuristics and 35.3% for the placement that is optimal against a shortest-path attacker. On the public GOADv2 collection, modelling AD CS abuse roughly doubles the undefended loot and changes the optimal placement; if traps can watch the final domain-takeover step, the chokepoint heuristic leaves 22 times the optimal loot with two traps. We also report a heuristic-evaluation bias of up to 1.96× and false optimality claims from an open-source MIP solver with presolve disabled.
